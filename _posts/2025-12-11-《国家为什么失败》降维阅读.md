# Preface

This book is about the huge differences in money and quality of life. These differences separate rich countries (like the US and UK) from poor ones (like those in **sub-Saharan** Africa).

We are writing this **preface**, which is a short introduction to the book. Right now, North Africa and the Middle East are shaking. This event is the "Arab Spring." It started with the "Jasmine Revolution."

This revolution was initially **ignited**, or sparked like a fire, by public anger. The people were angry about the **self-immolation** of a street seller named Mohamed Bouazizi. **Self-immolation** means he set himself on fire to kill himself.

By January 14, 2011, President Ben Ali had stepped down. He had ruled Tunisia since 1987. However, the movement was far from **abating**, which means it was not becoming weaker or stopping. Instead, the revolutionary **fervor**—the strong feelings of passion and excitement—was getting stronger. The people were fighting against the rule of **privileged elites**, the small group of rich people with special power.

This movement spread to the rest of the Middle East. Hosni Mubarak had ruled Egypt very tightly for thirty years. But he was **ousted**, or forced to leave his position, on February 11, 2011.

We are finishing this **preface** now. We do not know what will happen to the **regimes**, or the government systems, in Bahrain, Libya, Syria, and Yemen.

The roots of the **discontent** in these countries lie in their poverty. **Discontent** is a feeling of unhappiness and not being satisfied.

The average Egyptian earns much less than an American. Also, 20 percent of the population is in **dire** poverty. **Dire** means very serious, terrible, or urgent. These differences are big. However, they are actually small compared with the differences between the US and the poorest countries (like North Korea). In those places, more than half the people live in poverty.

Why is Egypt so poor? What are the **constraints**? A **constraint** is something that limits you or holds you back. These limits stop Egyptians from becoming **prosperous**, which means rich and successful.

We must ask: Is the poverty of Egypt **immutable**? If something is **immutable**, it is unchangeable and stays the same forever. Or, can the poverty be **eradicated**? To **eradicate** something means to remove or destroy it completely.

To understand this, we look at what Egyptians say. Noha Hamed, a worker in Cairo, explained why she fought against the Mubarak **regime** (government system). She said, “We are suffering from corruption and oppression.”

Mosaab El Shami, a student, **concurred**. This means he agreed with her completely. He said, “I hope we will have an elected government. I hope **universal** freedoms are applied.” **Universal** means relating to everyone in the world.

The protestors in Tahrir Square spoke about the government’s failure. They particularly complained about **repression**. **Repression** is the act of using force to control people and limit their freedom.

Mohamed ElBaradei wrote on Twitter that there was a “denial of **channels** for peaceful change.” Here, **channels** mean ways, paths, or methods to do something. He said this situation was a “ticking bomb.” Egyptians believed their economic problems were **fundamentally** caused by a lack of political rights. **Fundamentally** means at the most basic and important level.

The protestors started to **formulate** their demands. To **formulate** means to create or prepare a plan carefully. They did this **systematically**, which means in an organized way, step by step.

Interestingly, the first demands were all about political change. Money issues, like raising the minimum wage, were only **transitional** demands. **Transitional** refers to a period of change from one state to another. These economic demands were to be **implemented** later. To **implement** means to start using a plan or to put it into action.

However, the protestors in Tahrir Square have different ideas. They have sharply **diverged** from the **conventional wisdom**. To **diverge** means to move in a different direction or to disagree. **Conventional wisdom** refers to the opinions that most people or experts accept as true.

When most academics and commentators explain why Egypt is poor, they emphasize completely different factors.

Some stress that Egypt’s poverty is determined by its geography. They say the country is mostly a desert and lacks **adequate** rainfall. **Adequate** means enough or sufficient for a need. They argue that the climate does not allow **productive** agriculture, meaning farming that produces a large amount of food.

Others point to culture. They say some **cultural attributes** (qualities or characteristics) of Egyptians are **inimical** to development. If something is **inimical** to success, it is harmful or unfriendly to it. They argue that Egyptians lack a good work **ethic**—a belief in the moral value of hard work. They also claim that Islamic beliefs are **inconsistent** with success, which means they do not match or work well with economic growth.

A third approach is **dominant** among economists. If an idea is **dominant**, it is the most important or powerful one. This approach is popular among policy **pundits**. A **pundit** is an expert who gives opinions to the public.

This approach is based on a specific **notion**, or idea/belief. The **notion** is that Egypt's rulers simply don't know how to make the country rich. It assumes they have followed incorrect policies. The thinking goes like this: if rulers got the right advice, **prosperity** (wealth and success) would follow.

To these experts, the political situation does not matter. They ignore the fact that **narrow elites** are **feathering their nests**. To **feather your nest** is an idiom. It means to make yourself rich, often in a dishonest way, while you are in a powerful position. They think this is **irrelevant**, or not related, to the economic problems.

In this book, we will argue that the Egyptians in Tahrir Square have the right idea. Most academics and commentators are wrong.

In fact, Egypt is poor **precisely** because of its rulers. The word **precisely** means exactly or accurately. The country has been ruled by a narrow elite. These elites have organized society for their own benefit. They did this at the **expense** of the vast mass of people. If you do something at someone's **expense**, you gain something while causing them to lose or suffer.

Political power has been narrowly **concentrated**. When power is **concentrated**, it is gathered in one place or held by very few people. This power has been used to create great wealth for the rulers. For example, ex-president Mubarak apparently **accumulated** a $70 billion fortune. To **accumulate** means to gather or collect a large amount over time. The losers have been the Egyptian people, as they understand very well.

We will show that this **interpretation** (explanation or view) of Egyptian poverty is correct. It provides a general explanation for why poor countries are poor. Whether it is North Korea, Sierra Leone, or Zimbabwe, the reason is the same.

In contrast, countries like Great Britain and the United States became rich for a different reason. Their citizens **overthrew** the elites. To **overthrow** means to remove a leader or government from power by force. They created a society where political rights were broadly **distributed**, meaning shared among many people.

In these rich countries, the government was **accountable** to citizens. If a government is **accountable**, it must explain its actions and can be punished if it does wrong. It was also **responsive**, meaning it reacted quickly and positively to people's needs. Because of this, the mass of people could take advantage of economic opportunities.

We will show that to understand world inequality, we have to **delve into** the past. To **delve into** means to research or examine something deeply. We must study the historical **dynamics** of societies—the forces that produce change.

We will see that Britain is richer than Egypt because of a specific event. In 1688, Britain had a revolution. This revolution **transformed** the politics and economics of the nation. To **transform** means to change something completely and for the better.

People fought for more political rights. Then, they used these rights to expand their economic opportunities. The result was a **fundamentally** different path. **Fundamentally** means in a basic and important way. This new path, or **trajectory**, led to a great result. It was **culminating in** the Industrial Revolution. If a process is **culminating in** an event, it reaches its highest or final point with that event.

The Industrial Revolution created powerful new technologies. It **unleashed** these technologies into the world, like opening a cage to let a wild animal out. However, these changes did not spread to Egypt.

This was because Egypt was under the control of the **Ottoman Empire**. This empire treated Egypt badly. It acted in the same way the Mubarak family did later.

Later, Napoleon Bonaparte **overthrew** the Ottoman rule in 1798. To **overthrow** means to remove a leader by force. But then, the country fell under **British colonialism**. This means a foreign country (Britain) came in and took control of the land and government.

The British were like the Ottomans. They had little interest in **promoting** Egypt's success. To **promote** means to encourage or help something grow. They did not care about **prosperity**, or the wealth and well-being of the people.

Eventually, the Egyptians **shook off** the Ottoman and British empires. Imagine shaking heavy dust off your coat; they finally got rid of these foreign rulers. In 1952, they also removed their **monarchy**, a government ruled by a king.

However, these were not real revolutions like the one in England in 1688. They did not **fundamentally** change politics. **Fundamentally** means in a basic or complete way.

Instead, these changes just brought a new group of elites to power. These new rulers were **disinterested** in helping ordinary Egyptians. Unlike a caring leader, they were **disinterested**; they simply did not care about the people's success.

**In consequence**, or as a direct result, the basic structure of society did not change. Therefore, Egypt stayed poor.

In this book, we will study how these patterns **reproduce** themselves over time. To **reproduce** means to copy or make happen again; bad history often repeats itself.

We will also study why sometimes these patterns are **altered**. When something is **altered**, it is changed or made different. This happened in England in 1688 and in France with the revolution of 1789.

This will help us understand the situation in Egypt today. We want to know if the revolution that **overthrew** (removed by force) Mubarak will lead to a new set of **institutions**. **Institutions** are the important systems or organizations, like laws and government, that run a country. We hope these new systems are **capable of** bringing **prosperity** (wealth and success) to ordinary Egyptians.

Egypt has had revolutions in the past that did not change things. This is because the people who **mounted** the revolutions had a selfish goal. To **mount** a revolution means to organize and start it. However, these leaders simply took over the **reins** of power. Just like a rider holds the **reins** to control a horse, these new leaders took control of the country. They took power from those they had **deposed**. To **depose** means to remove a leader from their high position. Then, they **re-created** a similar bad system.

It is indeed difficult for ordinary citizens to **acquire** real political power. To **acquire** means to get or gain something important. It is hard to change the way their society works.

But it is possible. We will see how this happened in England, France, and the United States. We will also look at Japan, Botswana, and Brazil.

**Fundamentally** (basically), a political **transformation** is required. A **transformation** is a complete and big change. This sort of change is needed for a poor society to become rich.

There is evidence that this may be happening in Egypt. Reda Metwaly, another protestor in Tahrir Square, argued, “Now you see Muslims and Christians together, now you see old and young together, all wanting the same thing.”

We will see that such a broad movement in society was a key part of other successful **transformations**. If we understand when and why such **transitions** occur, we can learn. A **transition** is the process of changing from one state to another.

We will be in a better position to **evaluate** the future. To **evaluate** means to judge or calculate the quality or likely result of something. We can know when we expect such movements to fail, as they often have in the past. We can also know when we may hope that they will succeed and improve the lives of millions.

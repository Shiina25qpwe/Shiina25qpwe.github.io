# Test 1

## Reading Passage 1

### Modified Text: Children’s Ideas About Rainforests

**Part 1: How children learn about the problem**

Adults and children often face a serious topic. They are frequently **confronted with** news about the forests; they have to face these facts directly. The news talks about the **alarming** rate of forest loss. This speed is frightening and causes great worry.

For example, there is one **graphic** picture shown to kids. It is a visual image that shows the situation clearly. This is an **illustration**; it is an example that explains the point effectively. Children can **readily** understand this; they can do it easily and quickly. They can **relate** to it because they feel a personal connection to the image.

Experts make an **estimate**—a calculated guess—that forests are being destroyed very fast. The rate is **equivalent to** 1,000 football fields every 40 minutes; it is equal to that amount. This time is about the **duration** of a normal school class; it is the length of time the class lasts.

TV shows often present **vivid** pictures. These images are bright, clear, and look very real. Because of this frequent media **coverage** (news reporting), children form their own ideas. They form these ideas **independent of** school; they do this on their own, without help from teachers. They do not need **formal tuition**, or official teaching in class, to learn this. However, some of their ideas might be wrong.

**Part 2: How misunderstandings form in the mind**

Studies show that children **harbor** wrong ideas. They hold and keep these thoughts inside their minds. These are **misconceptions**; they are mistaken beliefs about science.

These wrong ideas are not **isolated**; they do not stand alone or separate from other thoughts. Instead, they become **incorporated into** a larger system; they get mixed and combined into the child's mind. This system is a **multifaceted** **framework**. It is a mental structure with many different sides and parts.

This framework has **component** ideas; these are the smaller parts that make up the whole. Some of these ideas are **erroneous**; they are incorrect and full of errors. The system is **robust**; it is strong and hard to break. However, it is also **accessible to** change; it is open and reachable for fixing. It needs **modification**, or small changes and adjustments.

Children develop these thoughts by **absorbing** ideas from TV; they take the information in like a sponge sucking up water. But schools may not let children express these ideas. If they did, the ideas could be **refined**; they could be improved and polished by teachers to be correct.

**Part 3: The goal of the study**

The media gives **extensive** reports on the forests; the information covers a large area and is very detailed. But we know little about what kids actually think. The goal of this study is to help teachers plan **strategies**. These are careful plans and methods to teach better. Teachers want to build on the correct ideas and **displace** the wrong ones; they want to remove the mistakes and put the truth in their place.

**Part 4: What the survey found**

The study asked children about **habitats**. These are the natural homes where plants and animals live.

The results showed that girls’ answers were **consistent with** past studies; their answers agreed with and matched the old results. We also looked at their views on **conservation**; this is the act of protecting and saving nature. Girls were more **sympathetic** to animals; they felt kind and sorry for them. They felt animals have **intrinsic** value; the value comes from inside the animals themselves, not just because humans use them.

**Part 5: Causes of destruction**

One result was good. **Encouragingly**, it gives us hope to see that most students knew the cause. They **identified** human activities; they recognized and named humans as the problem. Some students were **personalizing** the blame; they made it about themselves by saying "we are destroying it." About 18% mentioned **logging**; this is the specific business of cutting down trees for wood.

However, a **proportion** (a part or share) of the students blamed acid rain. Children are **confusing** different things; they are mixing up rainforests with forests in Europe. They think the **factors**, or causes, are the same.

Some students said forests provide oxygen. But this idea **embraced** a mistake; it included and held onto a wrong belief. They thought without rainforests, the air would be **incompatible with** human life; the atmosphere would not match what we need to survive.

**Part 6: Conclusion**

The results show that certain ideas **predominate**. These ideas are the strongest and most common in children's thinking. They have mistakes about **ecosystems**; these are the complex webs of living things and their environment. They also misunderstand **climatic** change; this relates to the long-term weather patterns.

Pupils did not **volunteer** complex ideas; they did not offer them willingly without being asked. They showed no **appreciation** of the problem's depth; they did not fully understand or recognize the value of it. They missed the **complexity**; they did not see the many complicated parts.

There was no **indication**, or sign, that they understood social factors. They did not know what factors **drive** the destruction; they did not know what forces push and cause the bad activities.

However, older children **acquire** skills; they gain and get these abilities over time. They can **evaluate** views; they can judge the quality of different opinions. They can handle **conflicting** views; they can look at ideas that fight against each other. Environmental education offers an **arena**; it provides a place or stage where these skills can grow.

## Reading Passage 2

### Modified Text: The Senses of Whales and Dolphins

**Part 1: Smell and Taste – The missing senses**

Humans and other **terrestrial** mammals live on land. We usually **take for granted** our ability to smell and taste; we assume we will always have these senses and use them without thinking. However, in **cetaceans** (the group that includes whales and dolphins), these senses are either missing or do not work well.

For example, look at the brain structure. It appears that toothed whales cannot smell. On the other hand, baleen whales have some brain parts for smell, but we do not know if they work. Scientists have not proven why, but it has been **speculated**. They have made a thoughtful guess that the change happened during evolution.

As the blowholes (noses) **migrated** to the top of the head—moving from the front to the top position—the nerves for smelling might have been lost. They were likely **sacrificed**; the animals gave up the sense of smell to gain the ability to breathe from the top of their heads. Similarly, some whales have taste buds, but the nerves serving them have **degenerated**. They have become weaker and smaller over time. These nerves are now **rudimentary**; they are undeveloped and almost useless, like a vestigial part.

**Part 2: The Sense of Touch – A surprising strength**

Some people think the sense of touch in whales is weak, but this idea is likely wrong. Trainers work with **captive** dolphins; these animals live in tanks and pools, not in the wild. The trainers often notice the animals’ **responsiveness**; the dolphins react quickly and clearly when they are rubbed.

This is true for **free-ranging** whales as well; unlike the ones in tanks, these whales swim freely in the open ocean. They touch each other often. This contact may help to **maintain order**; it keeps peace, organization, and discipline within the group. Also, touching is part of the **courtship ritual**. It is a behavior used to attract a mate and show love before breeding.

However, the area around the blowhole is very sensitive. If you touch them there, they might **object strongly**; they will show they dislike it and try to stop you.

**Part 3: Vision – How they see underwater**

Vision works differently in different species. Some baleen whales were studied **at close quarters**; the scientists were very near to them, not far away. The whales obviously **tracked** objects; they followed the movement of the objects with their eyes.

However, the position of their eyes limits them. They probably do not have **stereoscopic** vision. This means they cannot combine the images from two eyes to see depth or 3D, like humans can.

**Part 4: Vision – Dolphins and the water surface**

Dolphins are different. Their eye position suggests they have **stereoscopic** vision looking forward. The bottlenose dolphin has very sharp eyes. It watches flying fish. It can see well through the air–water **interface**; this is the boundary line where the water meets the air.

Scientific experiments are still **preliminary**; they are at the first, early stage and not finished. These early tests suggest their vision in air is bad. However, there is **anecdotal** evidence **to the contrary**. Stories told by trainers show the opposite is true: dolphins can jump and catch small fish from a hand, which requires great eyes.

**Part 5: Environment determines vision**

The difference in vision depends on the habitat. Vision is useful in clear water. But it is less useful in **turbid** rivers; the water there is muddy, cloudy, and hard to see through. For example, some river dolphins are blind. Their eyes can only sense the **intensity** of light; they know how strong or bright the sun is, but they cannot see shapes.

**Part 6: Hearing – The superpower**

The senses of smell and taste have **deteriorated**; they have become worse and broken down over time. However, this weakness is **compensated for** by their hearing. The amazing hearing makes up for the loss of other senses.

They have a well-developed **acoustic** sense; it relates to sound and hearing. Many whales **forage** for food using sound; they hunt and search for their prey by listening to echoes.

Baleen whales use low sounds. They are limited in their **repertoire**; the list of songs or sounds they can perform is short. A notable exception is the humpback whale. It produces complex **utterances**; it makes strange vocal sounds that act like a language.

Toothed whales use a wider **spectrum** of frequencies; they use the full range from low to high sounds. However, the sperm whale is different. It produces a **monotonous** series of clicks; the sound is repetitive, boring, and never changes tone.

Some sounds are clearly for communication. But what do they mean? Scientists are not sure. Theories about whale "culture" are mostly **speculation**; they are just wild guesses without solid proof.

## Reading Passage 3

### Modified Text: How Blind People "See" Drawings

**Part 1: Can the blind understand pictures?**

Recent studies show something interesting. It is clear that blind people can **appreciate**, or understand and enjoy, the use of outlines. They can understand **perspectives**; they can mentally see how objects are arranged in space, deciding which is near and which is far.

But pictures are more than just **literal** copies. They are not just exact, realistic copies of the real world. I realized this fact **dramatically**; it hit me with sudden and strong force.

It happened when a blind woman in my study decided to draw a spinning wheel. She did this **on her own initiative**; nobody asked her to do it, she chose to do it herself. To show the motion, she drew a curved line inside the circle. I was **taken aback**; I was very surprised and shocked because I did not expect it.

**Part 2: The history of motion lines**

Lines that show motion are a **recent** invention. In fact, an art scholar noted that Wilhelm Busch, a famous cartoonist, used almost no motion lines. He was a **trend-setting** artist; he started new styles that others followed. But even he did not use these lines until 1877.

I asked other blind people to draw a spinning wheel. One particularly clever **rendition** appeared many times; this version or performance of the drawing was common. They drew the spokes as curved lines.

When asked, they said these curves were **metaphorical**; they were not real curves, but symbols used to compare the wheel to something moving. The **majority rule** would argue that this worked; since most people did it, it must be a good way to show motion. But was this really a good **indicator**, or sign, of movement? Or were these just **idiosyncratic** marks—strange, personal marks that only one person understood?

I wanted to find out if these lines were **apt**; I wanted to know if they were suitable and appropriate for showing movement. Moreover, I wanted to see how the blind **interpreted** them; I wanted to know how they explained and understood the meaning of these lines.

**Part 3: The Experiment**

To find the answers, I made drawings with raised lines so the blind could feel them. I created five wheels. I was **depicting** the spokes in different ways; I was showing them as curved, bent, wavy, dashed, and extended.

I asked eighteen blind volunteers to feel the wheels. I asked them to **assign** a motion to each wheel; they had to choose a specific movement for each one. The choices were: **wobbling** (shaking unsteadily from side to side), spinning fast, spinning steadily, **jerking** (moving with sudden, sharp stops and starts), or braking.

My **control group** consisted of sighted students; this group was used for comparison to see the difference between blind and sighted people.

**Part 4: The Results**

Almost everyone found a meaning. They assigned **distinctive** motions; each wheel got a special, different movement.

- Most guessed curved spokes meant spinning steadily.
- Wavy spokes suggested the wheel was **wobbling**; it felt like it was shaking.
- Bent spokes were taken as a sign the wheel was **jerking**; it was moving roughly.
- Spokes extending beyond the **perimeter** (the outer edge or border) meant the brakes were on.
- Dashed spokes **signified** that the wheel was spinning fast; they acted as a sign or symbol for speed.

The results were amazing. The favorite description for the sighted was the same as for the blind in every **instance**; it happened in every single case or example.

What is more, the **consensus** among the sighted was similar to the blind. The consensus is the general agreement that everyone reaches.

Because motion lines are strange to the blind, the task involved problem-solving. **Evidently**, it is clearly seen that the blind figured it out. They generally came up with the same meaning as frequently as sighted people.

**Part 5: Abstract Symbols**

We found that the blind understand other **visual metaphors** too. One woman drew a picture of a child inside a heart. She chose that **symbol**—a shape that stands for an idea—to show love.

I began exploring the **symbolism** behind shapes; I studied the deeper meaning that shapes like hearts represent. We gave a list of word pairs to subjects. We asked them to pick the term that best **related to** a circle or a square; they had to choose which word had a connection with each shape.

All subjects **deemed** the circle soft; they judged and considered it to be soft. A full 94% **ascribed** "happy" to the circle; they gave or credited that quality to the shape.

However, other pairs **revealed** less agreement; they showed or uncovered that people did not agree as much. For example, 79% matched fast to slow. Only 51% **linked** deep to circle; they connected the two ideas together.

When we tested blind volunteers, their choices **closely resembled** the sighted ones; they looked very similar to the answers from people who could see. One man scored extremely well. He made only one match differing from the **consensus**. He put "far" with square.

Thus, we **concluded** that the blind interpret abstract shapes just like sighted people do; we reached this final decision based on the facts.

The dean studied the paper. "You are **utterly** right, boy. This shipment is a **wreck** of lies." He had to **distinguish** the true numbers from the fake ones.

Colonel Harris stared at Jack. Maybe his son did **possess** a special skill. It was not **strenuous** physical work, and not **ceremonial** drills. It was an **inborn** gift to see the truth. For one happy **instance**, his son had shown real **competence**. His smile became **infectious**. Everyone smiled too.

Later, Jack went back to his true **avocation**. He helped the real town postman carry parcels by the **bay**. The **snowdrift**s were deep, and **migratory** birds flew over the water. Jack used his old **shaver** radio to listen to **harmonic** tunes. Soft, pretty music.

His father no longer asked him to do things that felt like a **vacuum**—empty and joyless. Jack learned that you do not need a fancy title to lead a good life. You just need to be honest, never **mislead**, and never let anyone **wreck** your simple dream.

## 05

Tommy and his friend Leo were in the school’s old art room. The room was dusty and **solitary**, with no one else inside. It was so quiet they could hear their own breathing.

On a table, they found a box full of forgotten things. A note on top said, “Please fix these for the school show. —Mrs. Green.” Tommy felt **thankful** that Mrs. Green trusted them to help.

Inside the box, they saw a cloth with beautiful **embroidery** on it. Bright threads made flowers and birds all over the blue fabric. It must have taken someone hours.

Next, Leo pulled out a piece of **pumice**. It was a gray stone so light it almost floated in his hand. He rubbed it gently against the edge of a broken **tile**, and it smoothed the rough part away. “Cool, a rock that can scrape things clean,” Leo said.

Under the tile lay a drawing of an **owl**. The bird sat on a branch, its big eyes staring right at them.

“Look at this **pattern**,” Tommy said, pointing to the cloth. The same flower shape was repeated again and again. It helped hide a small torn spot.

They also found a **sumptuous** golden frame. It was so shiny and rich-looking that Leo whispered, “This looks like it costs a thousand dollars.”

Suddenly, a little girl walked in. She seemed nervous. “I’m sorry,” she said, her face taking on a **tinge** of pink. “I’m new here.”

“It’s okay,” Tommy said. He disliked **patronizing** people, so he didn’t speak to her like she was a silly baby. Instead, he showed her the box.

The girl’s name was Maya. She saw the drawings and said, “You can use **alliteration** in a show poster. Like ‘Big Blue Birds’ — same starting sound. It sounds fun.”

She pointed to a poem in the box. It read, “The silly snake slid slowly.” The s-sound made the sentence feel slippery and full of **frivolity** — just light, playful fun without a serious purpose.

Leo smiled. He had a **flair** for making people feel welcome. His natural talent showed when he said, “Want to help us fix things?” Maya nodded, looking happy.

First, they worked on a small fan. It was **blighted** by rust and dust, completely broken and useless. But the vent holes gave Tommy an idea.

“We need good **ventilation** for the art room,” he said. “Fresh air should move in and out, so we don’t get too hot.”

While fixing the fan, Leo touched the metal edge and cut his **shin** — the front part of his leg below the knee. He jumped back but said he was fine. Maya told him to be careful; it was a good **precaution** to wear long pants when handling old metal.

Next, they unwrapped a glass ball. Inside was a tiny **hydrosphere**: water and a little island, all sealed in. “It’s like a model of Earth’s water parts,” Maya said.

They discussed a plan to paint a **monochromatic** poster for the Earth Day booth — using only different shades of blue. Tommy thought it would look calm, **albeit** he admitted he still wanted some green. “Albeit” just means “even though,” he explained with a shrug.

Maya found a stamp with **entrenched** ink. The ink was stuck deep in the carved lines and wouldn’t come out no matter how much she scrubbed. It was there to stay.

Leo joked that they needed a **deity** of cleaning to help them — a god or goddess with magic powers. They laughed, but the cleaning wasn’t a **spontaneous** laugh; they started laughing right at the same moment without planning it.

Meanwhile, the sun outside caused **evaporation**. Puddles from the morning rain were slowly drying up, turning into water vapor they couldn’t even see. The process was **imperceptibly** happening — so slowly they didn’t notice it at all.

Their teacher, Mr. Dan, passed by the door. He paused and looked at the metal pieces. He said the rusty handles were from the old **armory**, where sports equipment and protective gear used to be stored. Now it was just a storage closet.

Mr. Dan added, “You’re doing good work. But **skimp** one part — do a careless, rushed job on it — and the whole thing might break.”

He reminded them not to bring any **undue** attention to the cracked wall. “Too much noise there isn’t necessary,” he said. “The main office is still on **probation** for that wall. They’re being watched carefully to make sure they follow rules before it can be fixed.”

Tommy didn’t quite understand. “Why is that a rule?”

Mr. Dan sighed. “It’s an old rule, **entrenched** since before I came. And it’s hard to change rules unless we propose an **amendment** — a formal change. But you can’t vote; you’re only in your **teens**, still kids.”

This made Tommy frown. “That might **deprive** us of fresh air in here,” he said, meaning it would stop them from getting what they needed. But he didn’t want to **insist** too much — demanding over and over sometimes just annoys people.

Maya, quiet until now, suddenly found a map. She gasped. “Look at this **trove**! A treasure collection!” The map pointed to a spot by the lake. It showed a **transferable** ticket — something that could be given from one person to another — to an **employee** party at the old nature shop. The shop name was a **syndicate**, a group of gardeners who worked together.

The map led them behind the gym, next to a huge old tree. There, **narrowly** missing stepping on a thorny vine — just barely avoiding it — Tommy found a stone box. Inside was a bar of handmade soap. The smell hit them right away: **fragrant**, like a garden full of roses. It was sweet and clean.

There was also a letter. It talked about soil that needed someone to **fertilize** it — to add food to help plants grow strong. The writer used **artifice**, a clever trick: she made the box look like a boring old stone so no one would find it.

The letter also warned that someone in the past had tried to **consume** all the garden’s resources — to use them all up selfishly. A **horde** of beetles, a huge crowd of them, had attacked the roses. But the **owl** in the tree ate many beetles and saved the day. The writer had a **pronounced**, very clear and strong, respect for owls after that.

Maya smiled. “What a find!” she said, holding the **fragrant** soap. They all agreed: this was the best afternoon ever.

“We learned more than in class,” Leo said.

Tommy nodded. They had learned hard words, **albeit** without even trying. The **TV** in the hallway flickered on, showing the news, but no one cared. They were too busy digging through their newfound **trove**.
